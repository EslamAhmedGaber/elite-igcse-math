    Set $u=x^2+k\gt 0$. Applying the quotient and chain rules,

    \[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{(2x/u)u-2x\ln u}{u^2} =\frac{2x(1-\ln(x^2+k))}{(x^2+k)^2}.\]

    Comparing with the required form identifies both constants.

    \[\boxed{A=2,\qquad B=1}\]
  2. Part (b)

    The denominator is positive, so stationary points satisfy

    \[x=0\quad\text{or}\quad \ln(x^2+k)=1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=0\quad\text{or}\quad x=\pm\sqrt{e-k}.\]

    There are three distinct real stationary points precisely when $e-k\gt 0$. In that case the factor $1-\ln(x^2+k)$ is positive between the outer roots and negative outside them; $2x$ changes sign at zero.     \[\boxed{x=0,\quad x=-\sqrt{e-k},\quad x=\sqrt{e-k}}\]

    \[\boxed{\text{Upper limit }e}\]

  1. Part (a)

    Differentiate x with respect to y by the product rule:

    \[\frac{dx}{dy}=e^{2y}+2ye^{2y}=e^{2y}(1+2y).\]

    Where $dx/dy\ne0$, take the reciprocal:

    \[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{e^{-2y}}{1+2y}.\]

    For $y\ne0$, the original relation gives $e^{2y}=x/y$, hence the required form $dy/dx=y/[x(1+2y)]$. At the origin, that last quotient is $0/0$; the preceding uncancelled derivative gives $dy/dx=1$. At $y=-1/2$ there is no finite $dy/dx$.

    \[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{y}{x(1+2y)}\quad\text{where defined}}\]
  2. Part (b)

    Consider $x=h(y)=ye^{2y}$. Its derivative $h'(y)=e^{2y}(1+2y)$ vanishes at $y=-1/2$. It is negative before this y value and positive after it, so the minimum x value is $h(-1/2)=-1/(2e)$. As $y\to-\infty$, $h(y)\to0$ from below; also $h(0)=0$ and h then increases without bound. Thus a level $x=k$ meets both negative-y branches precisely between the minimum and zero. At the minimum the two intersections coincide; at zero there is just the finite point $y=0$.

    \[\boxed{-\frac1{2e}\lt k\lt 0}\]
Final answer

(a) $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{y}{x(1+2y)}\quad\text{where defined}$ (b) $-\frac1{2e}\lt k\lt 0$
