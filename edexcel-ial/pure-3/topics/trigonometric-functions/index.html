1. WMA13/01 October 2025 · Q4

Medium6 marks · main topic: Trig Equations & R-Form
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    Expand $R\sin(2x-\alpha)=R\cos\alpha\sin2x-R\sin\alpha\cos2x$. Comparison gives $R\cos\alpha=\sqrt3$ and $R\sin\alpha=3$. Thus $R=\sqrt{3+9}=2\sqrt3$ and $\tan\alpha=\sqrt3$. As $0\lt \alpha\lt \pi/2$, $\alpha=\pi/3$ and

    \[f(x)=2\sqrt3\sin(2x-\pi/3).\]
  2. Part (b)(i)
    \[g(x)=\frac{18}{2\sqrt3\sin(6x-\pi/3)+4\sqrt3}.\]

    The denominator is always positive, between $2\sqrt3$ and $6\sqrt3$. The fraction is smallest when the denominator is largest:

    \[\min g=\frac{18}{6\sqrt3}=\sqrt3.\]
  3. Part (b)(ii)

    The maximum denominator occurs when $\sin(6x-\pi/3)=1$:

    \[6x-\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{\pi}{2}+2\pi n \Longrightarrow x=\frac{5\pi}{36}+\frac{\pi n}{3}.\]

    The smallest positive value is $x=5\pi/36$, since $n=-1$ gives a negative value.

Final answer

(a) $2*sqrt(3)*sin(2*x-pi/3)$ (b)(i) $sqrt(3)$ (b)(ii) $5*pi/36$

2. WMA13/01 January 2023 · Q2

Medium6 marks · main topic: Trig Equations & R-Form
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    Expand $R\cos(x-\alpha)=R\cos\alpha\cos x+R\sin\alpha\sin x$. Comparing coefficients gives $R\cos\alpha=1$ and $R\sin\alpha=2$. Thus $R^2=1^2+2^2=5$ and $\tan\alpha=2$. The specified first-quadrant phase is $\alpha=\arctan2=1.1071487\ldots$ radians.

    \[\boxed{R=\sqrt5,\qquad\alpha=1.107\ \text{radians}}\]
  2. Part (b)

    Using the exact phase $\alpha=\arctan2$, write $g(x)=3-7\sqrt5\cos(2x-\alpha)$. (i) The negative cosine coefficient means the maximum occurs when the cosine is $-1$; the maximum is $3+7\sqrt5$. (ii) $2x-\alpha=\pi+2n\pi$, so $x=(\pi+\alpha)/2+n\pi$. The $n=-1$ value is negative; $n=0$ is the smallest positive one, $x=2.1243706857\ldots$, or 2.12 to two decimal places.

    \[\boxed{\max g=3+7\sqrt5,\qquad x=2.12}\]
Final answer

(a) $R=\sqrt5,\qquad\alpha=1.107\ \text{radians}$ (b) $\max g=3+7\sqrt5,\qquad x=2.12$

3. WMA13/01 May/June 2025 · Q2

Medium7 marks · main topic: Trig Equations & R-Form
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    Expand $R\cos(x+\alpha)=R\cos\alpha\cos x-R\sin\alpha\sin x$. Comparing coefficients gives

    \[R\cos\alpha=7,\qquad R\sin\alpha=24.\]

    Hence

    \[R=\sqrt{7^2+24^2}=\boxed{25},\qquad \alpha=\arctan\frac{24}{7}=1.287002\ldots=\boxed{1.287\text{ radians}}.\]

    Thus $f(x)=25\cos(x+\alpha)$, using the unrounded $\alpha$ in subsequent calculations.

  2. Part (i)

    Since $-25\leq f(2x)\leq25$, the positive denominator $90-3f(2x)$ lies between $15$ and $165$. The smallest reciprocal occurs at the largest denominator:

    \[\boxed{g_{\min}=\frac5{165}=\frac1{33}}.\]
  3. Part (ii)

    The minimum requires $f(2x)=-25$, so

    \[\cos(2x+\alpha)=-1,\qquad 2x+\alpha=\pi+2n\pi.\]

    Since $0\lt \alpha\lt \pi/2$, the first positive solution uses $n=0$:

    \[x=\frac{\pi-\arctan(24/7)}2=0.927295\ldots=\boxed{0.927}.\]
Final answer

(a) $25$, $1.287\text{ radians}$ (i) $g_{\min}=\frac5{165}=\frac1{33}$ (ii) $0.927$ (b)(i) $g_{\min}=\frac5{165}=\frac1{33}$ (b)(ii) $\boxed{0.927}$

4. WMA13/01 October 2022 · Q2

Medium7 marks · main topic: Functions, Composition & Inverses
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    For $x\ge0$, $0\lt 4/(3x+2)\le2$. Thus $f(0)=3$ is attained, while $f(x)$ increases towards 5 without reaching it.

    \[\boxed{3\le f(x)\lt 5}\]
  2. Part (b)

    (i) Put $y=5-4/(3x+2)$. Rearranging gives $(5-y)(3x+2)=4$, hence

    \[x=\frac{4/(5-y)-2}{3}=\frac{2y-6}{15-3y}.\]

    Exchange variables. (ii) The inverse domain is the range of $f$ found above.

    \[\boxed{f^{-1}(x)=\frac{2x-6}{15-3x},\qquad3\le x\lt 5}\]
  3. Part (c)

    The original function has an absolute value and angle $x/3+\pi/6$:

    \[g(-\pi)=|4\sin(-\pi/3+\pi/6)|=|4\sin(-\pi/6)|=2.\]

    Then $fg(-\pi)=f(2)=5-4/(3\cdot2+2)=5-1/2=9/2$.

    \[\boxed{fg(-\pi)=\frac92}\]
Final answer

(a) $3\le f(x)\lt 5$ (b) $f^{-1}(x)=\frac{2x-6}{15-3x},\qquad3\le x\lt 5$ (c) $fg(-\pi)=\frac92$

5. WMA13/01 October 2021 · Q8

Medium7 marks · main topic: Differentiation Techniques
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    The principal arcsine curve increases from $(-2,-\pi/2)$ through $(0,0)$ to $(2,\pi/2)$. It is concave down on the negative-x half, concave up on the positive-x half, and vertical at both ends.

    \[\boxed{y=\arcsin(x/2),\quad -2\le x\le2}\]
  2. Part (b)

    Differentiate the supplied inverse relation: $dx/dy=2\cos y$. For $-\pi/2\lt y\lt \pi/2$, cosine is positive, so $\cos y=\sqrt{1-\sin^2y}=\sqrt{1-x^2/4}$. Taking the reciprocal gives

    \[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1{2\cos y}=\frac1{\sqrt{4-x^2}},\qquad -2\lt x\lt 2.\]

    Thus $A=4$; the endpoints have vertical tangents, not finite derivatives.

    \[\boxed{A=4,\qquad\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1{\sqrt{4-x^2}}}\]
  3. Part (c)

    At $y=\pi/4$, $x=2\sin(\pi/4)=\sqrt2$. The gradient is $1/\sqrt{4-2}=1/\sqrt2$. Hence $y-\pi/4=(x-\sqrt2)/\sqrt2$, so $y=x/\sqrt2+\pi/4-1$.

    \[\boxed{y=\frac{x}{\sqrt2}+\frac\pi4-1}\]
Final answer

(a) $y=\arcsin(x/2),\quad -2\le x\le2$ (b) $A=4,\qquad\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1{\sqrt{4-x^2}}$ (c) $y=\frac{x}{\sqrt2}+\frac\pi4-1$

6. WMA13/01 January 2020 · Q9

Medium7 marks · main topic: Trig Equations & R-Form
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    Expanding the required form gives

    \[R\cos(\theta+\alpha) =R\cos\alpha\cos\theta-R\sin\alpha\sin\theta.\]

    Comparing coefficients with \(5\cos\theta-4\sin\theta\),

    \[R\cos\alpha=5,\qquad R\sin\alpha=4.\]

    Squaring and adding, using \(R\gt 0\), gives

    \[R^2=5^2+4^2=41\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{R=\sqrt{41}}.\]

    Also, since \(0\lt \alpha\lt \frac\pi2\),

    \[\tan\alpha=\frac45,\qquad \alpha=\arctan\!\left(\frac45\right)=0.6747409\ldots \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\alpha=0.675\text{ radians (3 d.p.)}}.\]

    Thus the exact form is

    \[\boxed{f(\theta)=\sqrt{41}\cos\!\left(\theta+\arctan\!\frac45\right)}.\]
  2. Part (b)(i)

    A stretch parallel to the \(y\)-axis with scale factor \(\boxed{\sqrt{41}}\), taking \(y=\cos\theta\) to \(y=\sqrt{41}\cos\theta\).

  3. Part (b)(ii)

    A translation by the vector

    \[\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha\\0\end{pmatrix}} =\begin{pmatrix}-\arctan(4/5)\\0\end{pmatrix} \approx\begin{pmatrix}-0.675\\0\end{pmatrix}.\]

    This moves the curve \(\alpha\) units to the left, giving \(y=\sqrt{41}\cos(\theta+\alpha)\).

  4. Part (c)

    Since \(\theta\) can be any real number,

    \[-\sqrt{41}\leq f(\theta)\leq\sqrt{41} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0\leq(f(\theta))^2\leq41.\]

    Hence \(4\leq4+(f(\theta))^2\leq45\). Taking reciprocals of these positive bounds and multiplying by \(90\) gives

    \[\boxed{2\leq g(\theta)\leq\frac{45}{2}}.\]

    Both endpoints are attained: the minimum occurs when \(f(\theta)=\pm\sqrt{41}\), and the maximum when \(f(\theta)=0\).

Final answer

(a) $R=\sqrt{41}$, $\alpha=0.675\text{ radians (3 d.p.)}$, $f(\theta)=\sqrt{41}\cos\!\left(\theta+\arctan\!\frac45\right)$ (b)(i) $\sqrt{41}$ (b)(ii) $\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha\\0\end{pmatrix}$ (c) $2\leq g(\theta)\leq\frac{45}{2}$

7. WMA13/01 October 2021 · Q6

Medium8 marks · main topic: Differentiation Techniques
Show worked solution
  1. Part (i)

    Differentiate to obtain $y'=6x/(x^2-5)-8x$. At a stationary point in $x\gt \sqrt5$, x is nonzero, so $6/(x^2-5)=8$, giving $6=8x^2-40$ and $x^2=23/4$. The original positive domain selects $x=\sqrt{23}/2$, which exceeds $\sqrt5$. Hence $p=23$.

    \[\boxed{p=23,\qquad x=\frac{\sqrt{23}}2}\]
  2. Part (ii)

    (a) Differentiate the squared sine with the chain rule: $y'=4-24\sin x\cos x=4-12\sin2x$. Thus $A=4$, $B=-12$. (b) Since $-1\le\sin2x\le1$, the largest derivative is attained when $\sin2x=-1$: $4-12(-1)=16$.

    \[\boxed{A=4,\quad B=-12;\qquad\text{maximum gradient}=16}\]
Final answer

(i) $p=23,\qquad x=\frac{\sqrt{23}}2$ (ii) $A=4,\quad B=-12;\qquad\text{maximum gradient}=16$

8. WMA13/01 May/June 2021 · Q9

Medium8 marks · main topic: Trig Equations & R-Form
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    Expand $R\sin(x-\alpha)=R\cos\alpha\sin x-R\sin\alpha\cos x$. Therefore $R\cos\alpha=12$, $R\sin\alpha=5$. Thus $R=\sqrt{12^2+5^2}=13$ and $\alpha=\arctan(5/12)=0.3947911197\ldots$ radians, in the required first quadrant.

    \[\boxed{13\sin(x-0.395)\quad(R=13,\ \alpha=0.395\text{ rad})}\]
  2. Part (b)

    Write $g(\theta)=10+13\sin(2\theta-\pi/6-\alpha)$. (i) The minimum is $10-13=-3$. (ii) For sine to equal $-1$, $2\theta-\pi/6-\alpha=-\pi/2+2\pi n$. Hence $\theta=-\pi/6+\alpha/2+\pi n$. For $n=0$ this is negative, so the smallest permitted value uses $n=1$:

    \[\theta=\frac{5\pi}{6}+\frac12\arctan(5/12)=2.815389438\ldots\text{ radians}.\]

    Use the unrounded phase angle for the calculation.

    \[\boxed{\min g=-3;\qquad\theta_{\min}=\frac{5\pi}{6}+\frac12\arctan(5/12)}\]
  3. Part (c)

    As $\beta$ varies over the reals, $12\sin\beta-5\cos\beta$ covers $[-13,13]$. Its square therefore covers $[0,169]$, not $[169,169]$. Subtracting from 10 reverses the bounds, giving $[-159,10]$; both endpoints are attained.

    \[\boxed{-159\le h(\beta)\le10}\]
Final answer

(a) $13\sin(x-0.395)\quad(R=13,\ \alpha=0.395\text{ rad})$ (b) $\min g=-3;\qquad\theta_{\min}=\frac{5\pi}{6}+\frac12\arctan(5/12)$ (c) $-159\le h(\beta)\le10$

9. WMA13/01 January 2026 · Q9

Hard9 marks · main topic: Trig Equations & R-Form
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    Use $2\sin x\cos x=\sin2x$ and $2\sin^2x=1-\cos2x$:

    \[h(x)=8+3\sin x\cos x-6\sin^2x =5+\frac32\sin2x+3\cos2x.\]

    Expanding $R\sin(2x+\alpha)$ and comparing coefficients gives

    \[R\cos\alpha=\frac32,\qquad R\sin\alpha=3.\]

    Therefore

    \[P=5,\qquad R=\sqrt{\frac94+9}=\frac{3\sqrt5}{2}, \qquad\alpha=\arctan(2)=1.107\ \text{radians (3 d.p.)}.\]

    Thus $h(x)=5+\frac{3\sqrt5}{2}\sin(2x+\alpha)$ with the exact angle $\alpha=\arctan(2)$.

  2. Part (b)

    The given interval includes two complete periods. The range is

    \[\left[5-\frac{3\sqrt5}{2},\ 5+\frac{3\sqrt5}{2}\right].\]
  3. Part (c)

    At a minimum, $\sin(2x+\alpha)=-1$, so

    \[2x+\alpha=\frac{3\pi}{2}+2\pi n, \qquad x=\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac\alpha2+\pi n.\]

    On $0\le x\le2\pi$, $n=0$ and $n=1$ give the two minima. The larger coordinate is

    \[x_M=\frac{7\pi}{4}-\frac12\arctan(2)=4.944212785\ldots.\]
    \[x_M=4.94\quad\text{(3 significant figures)}.\]
Final answer

(a) $P=5,\ R=\frac{3\sqrt5}{2},\ \alpha=\arctan2$ (b) $\left[5-\frac{3\sqrt5}{2},\ 5+\frac{3\sqrt5}{2}\right]$ (c) $x_M=4.94$

10. WMA13/01 May/June 2024 · Q4

Hard9 marks · main topic: Trig Identities & Exact Values
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    Use $\sin2x=2\sin x\cos x$ and $2\cos^2x=1+\cos2x$:

    \[f(x)=4\sin2x+2(1+\cos2x)-3=4\sin2x+2\cos2x-1.\]
    \[\boxed{a=4,\quad b=2,\quad c=-1}\]
  2. Part (b)

    Expanding $R\sin(2x+\alpha)$ and matching coefficients gives

    \[R\cos\alpha=4,\qquad R\sin\alpha=2.\]

    Squaring and adding, $R^2=20$, so $R=2\sqrt5$ since $R\gt 0$. Also $\tan\alpha=1/2$, and $0\lt \alpha\lt \pi/2$ selects $\alpha=\arctan(1/2)=0.463647609\ldots$ radians. Thus $f(x)=2\sqrt5\sin(2x+\arctan(1/2))-1$. With the phase displayed to 3 significant figures, $f(x)\approx2\sqrt5\sin(2x+0.464)-1$.

    \[\boxed{R=2\sqrt5,\quad\alpha=0.464\ \text{radians},\quad c=-1}\]
  3. Part (c)

    (i) The sine term has maximum 1, hence the maximum of $f$ is $2\sqrt5-1$. (ii) Maxima occur when

    \[2x+\alpha=\frac\pi2+2k\pi, \qquad x=\frac\pi4-\frac\alpha2+k\pi.\]

    The value for $k=0$ is $0.553574\ldots\gt 0$, while $k=-1$ is negative. The second positive value therefore has $k=1$:

    \[x=\frac{5\pi}{4}-\frac12\arctan(1/2)=3.695167\ldots.\]
    \[\boxed{\max f=2\sqrt5-1,\quad x=3.70\ \text{radians}}\]
Final answer

(a) $a=4,\quad b=2,\quad c=-1$ (b) $R=2\sqrt5,\quad\alpha=0.464\ \text{radians},\quad c=-1$ (c) $\max f=2\sqrt5-1,\quad x=3.70\ \text{radians}$

  1. Part (a)

    Expand $R\sin(x-\alpha)=R\cos\alpha\sin x-R\sin\alpha\cos x$. Thus $R\cos\alpha=8$, $R\sin\alpha=15$, so $R=\sqrt{64+225}=17$ and $\alpha=\arctan(15/8)=1.0808390005\ldots$ radians, in the required quadrant.

    \[\boxed{R=17,\qquad\alpha=1.081\ \text{radians}}\]
  2. Part (b)

    Using the exact phase from (a),

    \[f(x)=\frac{15}{41+34\sin(x-\alpha)}.\]

    The denominator lies between 7 and 75, so it is always positive. (i) The smallest fraction occurs at its largest denominator: $f_{\min}=15/75=1/5$. (ii) This occurs when $x-\alpha=\pi/2+2n\pi$. The smallest positive value is $x=\alpha+\pi/2=2.6516353273\ldots$, or 2.65 to three significant figures.

    \[\boxed{f_{\min}=\frac15,\qquad x=2.65}\]
  3. Part (c)

    The transformation $2f(x)-5$ preserves the locations of minima and maps their y coordinate to $2(1/5)-5=-23/5$.

    \[\boxed{y=-\frac{23}{5}}\]
  4. Part (d)

    Reflection in the x-axis changes minima of f into maxima of $-f$. The argument $2x$ halves their x coordinates, so the first positive maximum is at

    \[x=\frac{\alpha+\pi/2}{2}=1.3258176637\ldots\approx1.33.\]
    \[\boxed{x=1.33}\]

12. WMA13/01 October 2020 · Q7

Hard9 marks · main topic: Trig Equations & R-Form
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    Expand $R\cos(x-\alpha)=R\cos\alpha\cos x+R\sin\alpha\sin x$. Matching coefficients gives $R\cos\alpha=1$, $R\sin\alpha=4$. Thus $R=\sqrt{1^2+4^2}=\sqrt{17}$ and $\tan\alpha=4$. Since $0\lt \alpha\lt \pi/2$, $\alpha=\arctan4=1.32581766\ldots$ radians.

    \[\boxed{R=\sqrt{17},\qquad\alpha=1.326\text{ radians}}\]
  2. Part (b)

    The model becomes $H=24/[3+\sqrt{17}\cos(t/2-\alpha)]$. For $0\le t\le6.5$, the phase lies between $-\alpha$ and $3.25-\alpha$; throughout this interval the denominator remains positive. Its maximum $3+\sqrt{17}$ is attained at $t=2\alpha=2.651635\ldots$, inside the time interval. The height is then smallest:

    \[H_{\min}=\frac{24}{3+\sqrt{17}}=3.369316876\ldots\ \mathrm m.\]

    To the nearest centimetre this is $3.37\ \mathrm m$ (337 cm).

    \[\boxed{H_{\min}=3.37\ \mathrm m}\]
  3. Part (c)

    For $H=10$, $3+\sqrt{17}\cos(t/2-\alpha)=24/10$, so

    \[\cos(t/2-\alpha)=-\frac{3}{5\sqrt{17}}.\]

    Let $\gamma=\arccos[-3/(5\sqrt{17})]$. In the permitted phase interval $[-\alpha,3.25-\alpha]$, only $+\gamma$ occurs: $-\gamma\lt -\alpha$, and shifts by $2\pi$ are outside it. Using the unrounded $\alpha=\arctan4$,

    \[t=2(\alpha+\gamma)=6.085307854\ldots\ \mathrm s.\]
    \[\boxed{t=6.09\ \mathrm s}\]
Final answer

(a) $R=\sqrt{17},\qquad\alpha=1.326\text{ radians}$ (b) $H_{\min}=3.37\ \mathrm m$ (c) $t=6.09\ \mathrm s$

13. WMA13/01 May/June 2024 · Q9

Hard10 marks · main topic: Applications of Differentiation
Show worked solution
  1. Part (a)

    At $y=\pi/3$, $x=4(\sqrt3/2)^2-1=3-1=2$.

    \[\boxed{k=2}\]
  2. Part (b)

    (i) Differentiating with respect to $y$ gives

    \[\frac{dx}{dy}=8\sin y\cos y.\]

    (ii) For $0\lt y\lt \pi/2$, both sine and cosine are positive, and $-1\lt x\lt 3$. From $x+1=4\sin^2y$,

    \[\sin y=\frac{\sqrt{x+1}}2,\qquad \cos y=\sqrt{1-\frac{x+1}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{3-x}}2.\]

    Consequently $dx/dy=2\sqrt{x+1}\sqrt{3-x}$. Taking the reciprocal proves the required formula on $-1\lt x\lt 3$. At $x=-1$ and $x=3$ the denominator is zero: these are endpoint vertical tangents, not finite derivatives.

    \[\boxed{\frac{dx}{dy}=8\sin y\cos y,\qquad\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1{2\sqrt{x+1}\sqrt{3-x}}}\]
  3. Part (c)

    At $P=(2,\pi/3)$ the tangent gradient is $1/(2\sqrt3)$, so the normal gradient is $-2\sqrt3$. Thus the normal is $y-\pi/3=-2\sqrt3(x-2)$. Setting $y=0$ gives $N=(2+\pi/(6\sqrt3),0)$. Triangle OPN has horizontal base $ON=2+\pi/(6\sqrt3)$ and perpendicular height $\pi/3$:

    \[\operatorname{Area}(OPN)=\frac12\left(2+\frac\pi{6\sqrt3}\right)\frac\pi3 =\frac\pi3+\frac{\pi^2}{36\sqrt3}.\]
    \[\boxed{\operatorname{Area}(OPN)=\frac13\pi+\frac{\sqrt3}{108}\pi^2}\]
Final answer

(a) $k=2$ (b) $\frac{dx}{dy}=8\sin y\cos y,\qquad\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac1{2\sqrt{x+1}\sqrt{3-x}}$ (c) $\operatorname{Area}(OPN)=\frac13\pi+\frac{\sqrt3}{108}\pi^2$
